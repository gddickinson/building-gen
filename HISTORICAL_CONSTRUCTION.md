# Historical Building Construction Reference

Comprehensive reference of period-correct construction techniques, materials, and dimensions
for use in the procedural building generator. All measurements verified against multiple
historical and conservation sources.

---

## 1. MEDIEVAL (1000-1500)

### 1.1 Wall Construction

#### Rubble Stone Walls
- **Construction**: Two outer faces of dressed or semi-dressed stone, core packed with rubble and lime mortar
- **Domestic wall thickness**: 600-900mm (2-3 ft) for houses
- **Castle curtain walls**: 2.5-6m (8-20 ft) thick; major royal castles (e.g. Dover) up to 6m (20 ft)
- **Church/cathedral walls**: up to 3.6m (12 ft)
- **Ashlar facing**: Precisely cut stone blocks with fine 3-6mm mortar joints
- **Rubble core**: Irregular stone pieces with 6-16mm mortar joints depending on dressing quality

Sources: [Medieval Wall Construction - Designing Buildings](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Medieval_wall_construction), [Castle Wall Thickness - Shieldmarch](https://shieldmarch.com/castle-wall-thickness-and-strength/), [Castle Architecture](https://www.castlesandmanorhouses.com/architecture_03_walls.htm)

#### Lime Mortar
- **Composition**: Quicklime (from burnt limestone/chalk) slaked with water to form lime putty, mixed with sand
- **Typical ratio**: ~1 part lime putty : 1.5 parts sand (equivalent to ~1 part dry quicklime : 3 parts sand)
- **Joint sizes**: Ashlar: 3-6mm; Coursed rubble: 10-12mm; Uncoursed rubble: up to 16mm
- **Setting time**: Extremely slow (carbonation process); limited daily build height; vulnerable to rain during setting
- **Compressive strength**: 50-250 psi (0.3-1.7 MPa) for historical lime mortars
- **Properties**: High vapour permeability, flexible, self-healing through continued carbonation

Sources: [Lime Mortar - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_mortar), [The Myth in the Mix - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/mythmix/mythmix.htm), [Lime Mortar vs Portland Cement - Craftsman Blog](https://thecraftsmanblog.com/lime-mortar-vs-portland-cement/)

#### Foundations
- **No separate foundation material**: Walls simply built wider at the base (stepped footings)
- **No DPC**: Walls built thick enough that damp would not penetrate to the interior
- **Moisture management**: Lime mortar joints more porous than stone, shedding moisture by evaporation
- **Capillary rise**: Accepted as inevitable; thick walls and good ventilation were the primary defences

Sources: [Damp Movement in Rubble Walls - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/damp-movement/damp-movement.htm), [Permagard Guide](https://permagard.co.uk/advice/damp-proofing-period-properties)

### 1.2 Timber Construction

#### Green Oak Framing
- **Material**: Green (unseasoned) oak, easier to work with hand tools
- **Typical post sizes**: 150-200mm (6-8 in) square for principal posts
- **Main beams**: 200x250mm (8x10 in) or larger for tie beams and main spans
- **Common rafters**: 100x125mm (4x5 in) to 125x150mm (5x6 in)
- **Purlins**: 100x150mm to 100x250mm (4x6 in to 4x10 in)
- **Joist sizes**: 150x200mm (6x8 in) for longer spans; smaller for secondary floors
- **Joist spacing**: 300-600mm (12-24 in) centres

#### Joints
- **Primary joint**: Mortise and tenon, secured with dry oak tapered pegs
- **Tenon thickness**: Approximately 25% of nominal beam width
- **Peg material**: Clear, straight, seasoned hardwood (oak, locust, maple)
- **Other joints**: Lap joints, scarf joints, dovetails for specific applications
- **Key property**: Green timber joints tighten as wood dries, strengthening over time

Sources: [Standard Timber Frame Joints](https://jointandpeg.com/timber-frame-joinery/standard-timber-frame-joints/), [Sessile Oak Workshop](https://sessile-oak.co.uk/oakFrames/carpentry), [Timber Frame HQ](https://timberframehq.com/fully-house-mortise-and-tenon-joint/)

### 1.3 Floors

| Type | Material | Details |
|------|----------|---------|
| Ground floor (common) | Beaten earth/clay | Compacted, sometimes mixed with straw or chalk; covered with rushes |
| Ground floor (status) | Stone flags | Set on compacted sand or mortar bed; variable sizes |
| Upper floors | Oak timber | Joists 150x200mm+ spanning between walls; rough-sawn or hand-planed oak planks nailed/pegged on top |

- **Achievable timber spans**: 3-5m (10-16 ft) typical for domestic; longer spans required intermediate beams/posts
- **Floorboard width**: Wide boards, 200-300mm (8-12 in), rough-sawn

Sources: [Medieval Floors - A Writer's Perspective](https://aprilmunday.wordpress.com/2017/03/12/the-medieval-floor/), [Traditional Solid Ground Floors - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/solid-ground-floors/solid-ground-floors.htm)

### 1.4 Roofs

| Material | Weight | Pitch | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|-------|
| Thatch (straw/reed) | ~30 kg/m2 | 45-60 deg | Up to 300mm thick; overhang eaves by ~900mm (3 ft) |
| Stone slate | ~75 kg/m2 | 30-45 deg | Regional (Cotswolds, Cumbria); heavy, requires robust timbers |
| Clay tiles | ~55 kg/m2 | 35-45 deg | Regulated 1477: 267x159x16mm (10.5x6.25x0.625 in) |
| Lead sheet | ~25 kg/m2 (Code 5) | 5-15 deg | Used on churches/cathedrals; small sheets/tiles; ~2.2mm thick |

- **Timber structure**: Rafter couples joined by collar beam; common rafters at ~400-600mm centres
- **Roof forms**: Simple coupled rafters (early); crown-post and collar-purlin (13th-14th C); side-purlin trusses (late 15th C)

Sources: [Weald & Downland Roofing Materials](https://www.wealddown.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Roofing-Materials.pdf), [Looking at Buildings: Timber Roofs](http://www.lookingatbuildings.org.uk/styles/medieval/roofs-and-vaults/timber-roofs.html), [Building Conservation - Metal Sheet Roofing](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/metal-sheet-roofing/metal-sheet-roofing.htm)

### 1.5 Windows

- **Pre-13th century**: Unglazed openings with wooden shutters; oiled/waxed linen cloth; scraped animal horn panes
- **13th century onward**: English glass industry develops (Surrey-Sussex border, Staffordshire)
- **Glazed windows**: Small panes set in lead came latticework; reserved for high-status buildings
- **Domestic glass**: Rare and expensive until 15th-16th century
- **Church glass**: Richly coloured stained glass from 12th century
- **Typical opening sizes**: Small -- 300-600mm (12-24 in) wide; often with stone mullions in better buildings

Sources: [Historic England - Medieval Glazing](https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/research/back-issues/the-origins-and-use-of-medieval-glazing-in-england/), [Windows Glass Glazing History - NBS](https://www.thenbs.com/knowledge/windows-glass-glazing-a-brief-history)

### 1.6 Doors

- **Construction**: Plank-and-batten (vertical planks with horizontal battens behind)
- **Hinges**: Iron strap hinges, pintle hinges
- **Typical sizes**: 1800-2000mm (6-6.5 ft) tall, 750-900mm (2.5-3 ft) wide for domestic
- **Castle doors**: Much larger; often with iron studs and reinforcement

### 1.7 Castle-Specific Features

#### Arrow Loops (Arrowslits)
- **External opening**: Narrow slit, ~50-100mm (2-4 in) wide
- **Internal embrasure**: Widens to ~600-900mm (2-3 ft) behind the slit in a triangular splay
- **Height**: ~1-2m (3-6.5 ft); taller for longbowmen (standing), shorter for crossbowmen
- **Variations**: Simple slit; cross-shaped (cruciform); fishtail (widened at base for ground visibility)

#### Crenellations (Battlements)
- **Merlons** (solid upstanding portions): Typically ~600mm (2 ft) wide, ~1.2m (4 ft) high
- **Crenels** (gaps): Typically one-third the width of the merlon (~200mm / 8 in)
- **Merlons could include arrow loops**

Sources: [Arrowslit - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowslit), [Battlement - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battlement), [Great Castles - Anatomy](https://great-castles.com/anatomy.html)

## 2. TUDOR / ELIZABETHAN (1500-1600)

### 2.1 Timber Frame with Infill

#### Frame Construction
- **Primary frame**: Large oak posts and beams forming the structural skeleton
- **Studs**: Vertical infill timbers between main posts, typically at 400-600mm centres
- **Characteristic feature**: Half-timbered appearance with visible frame and contrasting infill panels

#### Wattle and Daub Infill
- **Wattle**: Thin oak staves (uprights) interwoven with hazel withies (horizontal strips)
- **Daub**: Mixture of clay, animal dung, chopped straw, and sometimes lime
- **Panel thickness**: ~75-100mm (3-4 in) between timber faces
- **Later alternatives**: Brick nogging (bricks laid between studs, often in herringbone pattern)

Sources: [Wattle and Daub - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/wattleanddaub/wattleanddaub.htm), [Surveying Property: Tudor Houses](http://surveyingproperty.blogspot.com/2013/12/tudor-houses-timber-frame-construction.html)

### 2.2 Jettied Upper Floors

- **Overhang dimension**: Up to 600mm (2 ft) per floor
- **Construction**: Floor joists of upper storey project beyond the wall below; the jetty plate sits on projecting joist ends
- **Purpose**: Gained extra space on upper floors without obstructing narrow medieval streets
- **Structural benefit**: Weight of upper floors on cantilevered joists counterbalances the internal span

Sources: [Jettying - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jettying)

### 2.3 Early Brick

- **Tudor brick dimensions**: 210-250mm x 100-120mm x 40-50mm (variable, handmade)
- **1571 charter standardisation**: 229 x 114 x 57mm (9 x 4.5 x 2.25 in)
- **Mortar joints**: Thick, 15-25mm, to accommodate irregular brick shapes
- **Bonding**: Mainly English bond and English cross-bond
- **Decorative technique**: Diaper patterns using vitrified (over-burnt) headers creating dark purple/slate patterns
- **Characteristics**: Rounded edges, irregular sizes, deep colour variations from wood-fired kilns

Sources: [Tudor Brickwork - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/tudor-brickwork/tudor-brickwork.htm), [Bricks Through History (PDF)](https://www.brocross.com/Bricks/Penmorfa/Bricks/Bits/BRICKS%20THROUGH%20HISTORY.pdf)

### 2.4 Chimneys

- **Introduction**: Chimneys become common in domestic buildings during Tudor period
- **Typical fireplace**: ~1380mm wide x 910mm tall x 1000mm deep (4.5 x 3 x 3.3 ft)
- **Construction**: Brick, often with decorative twisted or moulded chimney stacks
- **Chimney stacks**: Clustered groups with individually decorated columns
- **Interior coating**: Lime or plaster lining (later recognised as fire hazard; Elizabeth I fire codes)
- **Flue section**: Roughly 230x230mm (9x9 in) for typical domestic fireplace

Sources: [Tudor Architecture - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_architecture), [History of Chimneys - Smith Heritage](https://smithheritagesurveyors.com/a-brief-history-of-chimneys-chimney-stacks-and-chimneys-pots/)

### 2.5 Glass

- **Crown glass**: Available but expensive; small panes set in lead cames
- **Leaded lights**: Diamond or rectangular panes, ~75-100mm (3-4 in) across
- **Glass thickness**: ~1.5-2.5mm (variable across each pane due to crown process)
- **Window openings**: Larger than medieval, with stone or timber mullions and transoms
- **Status indicator**: Number and size of glass windows indicated wealth

### 2.6 Roofs

- **Clay tiles**: Widespread; plain tiles ~265x165mm (10.5x6.5 in)
- **Pitch**: 40-55 degrees for plain tiles
- **Thatch**: Still common in rural areas
- **Lead**: For high-status buildings, churches

## 3. GEORGIAN (1714-1830)

### 3.1 Brick Construction

#### Brick Dimensions
- **Standard size**: ~229 x 114 x 65mm (9 x 4.5 x 2.5 in) in early Georgian
- **1784 brick tax era**: Sizes increased to ~254 x 127 x 76mm (10 x 5 x 3 in) to reduce number of taxed bricks
- **Treasury cap imposed**: Maximum 76mm (3 in) height, 254mm (10 in) length, 127mm (5 in) width
- **Mortar joints**: ~10mm typical

#### Wall Construction
- **Solid walls** (no cavity): All Georgian walls are solid brick
- **Ground floor**: 2 bricks thick = ~460mm (18 in) including mortar
- **First floor**: 1.5 bricks thick = ~345mm (13.5 in)
- **Upper floors**: Reduced by half-brick per storey; top floor may be 1 brick thick = ~230mm (9 in)
- **Bonding pattern**: Flemish bond (alternating headers and stretchers in each course) is the defining Georgian pattern
- **No DPC**: Georgians relied on good ventilation and thick walls to manage moisture

#### Mortar
- **Type**: Lime mortar throughout (Portland cement not yet available)
- **Composition**: ~1:3 lime putty to sand
- **Properties**: Soft, flexible, vapour-permeable; allows building to breathe

Sources: [Imperial Bricks - Sizes Guide](https://www.imperialbricks.co.uk/guidance/everything-you-need-to-know-about-imperial-brick-sizes/), [Flemish Bond - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flemish_bond), [Bricks Through History](https://www.brocross.com/Bricks/Penmorfa/Bricks/Bits/BRICKS%20THROUGH%20HISTORY.pdf)

### 3.2 Sash Windows

#### Configuration
- **Pattern**: Six-over-six (6 panes upper sash, 6 panes lower sash = 12 total)
- **Overall dimensions**: 700-1000mm wide x 1500-2100mm tall
- **Ground floor windows**: Up to 2400mm tall
- **Upper floors**: Progressively shorter windows for balanced facade

#### Crown Glass Panes
- **Individual pane size**: ~250-300mm wide x 350-400mm tall (10-12 x 14-16 in)
- **Maximum crown glass pane**: ~560mm (22 in) -- limited by radius of blown crown disc
- **Glass thickness**: ~1.5-2.5mm; varies within each pane (thicker at centre of crown disc)
- **Properties**: Brilliant surface, slight impurities, irregular texture give characteristic reflective quality

#### Glazing Bars
- **Early Georgian**: Thick oak bars, up to 38mm (1.5 in) wide
- **Mid Georgian**: 18-20mm (0.7-0.8 in) wide, typically softwood (Deal/Baltic pine)
- **Late Georgian**: As slim as 12-15mm (0.5-0.6 in)

#### Mechanism
- Counterbalanced by lead or cast-iron sash weights concealed in box frame

Sources: [Georgian Sash Windows Design Guide](https://timberwindows-direct.co.uk/georgian-windows-the-definitive-6-over-6-design-guide/), [Parsons Joinery - Georgian Windows](https://parsonsjoinery.com/blog/georgian-windows), [Sash Window - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sash_window)

### 3.3 Roof Construction

#### Welsh Slate
- **Availability**: Quarried at scale from mid-18th century (Penrhyn quarry naming system 1738)
- **Named sizes** (General Hugh Warburton system):
  - Lady: 406x203mm (16x8 in)
  - Viscountess: 457x229mm (18x9 in)
  - Countess: 508x254mm (20x10 in) -- most common
  - Marchioness: 559x279mm (22x11 in)
  - Duchess: 610x305mm (24x12 in)
- **Weight**: ~25-35 kg/m2 depending on size
- **Pitch**: 25-35 degrees (lower pitch than tiles due to impermeability)

#### Timber Structure
- **Rafters**: Typically 75x50mm to 100x75mm (3x2 to 4x3 in) softwood at 400mm centres
- **Purlins**: 175x75mm to 225x75mm (7x3 to 9x3 in) spanning between party walls

Sources: [Welsh Slate Sizes](https://www.welshslate.com/roofing/architects-and-specifiers/slate-sizes-and-weights/), [History House - Roof Slates](https://historyhouse.co.uk/articles/roof_slates.html)

### 3.4 Floor Construction

- **Joists**: Softwood (Baltic pine/fir), typically 200x50mm to 225x75mm (8x2 to 9x3 in)
- **Joist spacing**: 350-400mm (14-16 in) centres
- **Spans**: 3-4.5m (10-15 ft) typical domestic
- **Floorboards**: Softwood (pine/fir), 150-225mm (6-9 in) wide, ~25mm (1 in) thick
- **Ceiling below**: Lath and plaster (see 3.5)

Sources: [Evolution of Building Elements - UWE](https://fet.uwe.ac.uk/conweb/house_ages/elements/section4.htm), [Timber Floors - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/timberfloor/timberfloor.htm)

### 3.5 Lime Plaster System

#### Three-Coat System on Lath
1. **Scratch coat**: 6mm thick; 1:3 lime putty:sand with horsehair reinforcement
2. **Floating/brown coat**: 6mm thick; 1:3 lime putty:sand with horsehair
3. **Setting/finish coat**: 3mm thick; 3:2 lime putty:sand (no hair)
- **Total thickness**: ~15mm (0.6 in)

#### Lath
- **Material**: Riven (split) softwood laths
- **Dimensions**: ~25mm (1 in) wide, ~6mm (0.25 in) thick
- **Spacing**: 8-10mm (3/8 in) gaps between laths for plaster key (nibs)

#### Horsehair Reinforcement
- **Purpose**: Controls shrinkage cracking; bridges gaps; holds plaster nibs together
- **Dosage**: Handful per bucket of plaster mix
- **Material**: Typically horse tail or mane hair, ~50-75mm (2-3 in) long

Sources: [Plaster Restorations - Historic Plastering](https://www.plasterrestorations.com/historic-plastering-using-traditional-sand-lime-horsehair-wooden-lath-materials/), [Lime Plaster Reinforcement - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/limehair/limehair.htm)

### 3.6 Decorative Features

#### Skirting Boards
- **Height**: 170-285mm (6.7-11.2 in); grander rooms used taller skirtings
- **Thickness**: 20-55mm (0.8-2.2 in)
- **Profile**: Simple ovolo or ogee mouldings
- **1730 example**: 283mm height x 54mm width for high-status rooms

#### Cornices
- **Typical projection x depth**: 100x100mm to 200x170mm
- **High-status example**: 192x168mm with egg-and-dart and modillion blocks
- **Material**: Lime plaster, run in-situ using templates

#### Dado Rails
- **Height from floor**: ~900mm (3 ft) -- roughly chair-back height
- **Profile depth**: ~25-40mm

Sources: [Georgian Skirting - Atkey & Co](https://catalogue.atkeyandco.com/catalogue/georgian-period-skirting-board-gsk0200/), [Georgian Interior Mouldings - MDF Skirting Mouldings](https://www.mdfskirtingmouldings.co.uk/blogs/news/georgian-interior-mouldings), [Georgian Group - Mouldings (PDF)](https://georgiangroup.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/MOULDINGS.pdf)

## 4. VICTORIAN (1837-1901)

### 4.1 Brick and Mortar

#### Brick Dimensions
- **Standard Victorian**: ~229 x 108 x 76mm (9 x 4.25 x 3 in)
- **London stock brick**: Characteristic yellow-brown colour
- **Machine-made bricks** (from ~1850s): More uniform, consistent sizing
- **Handmade bricks**: Still used; compressive strength ~18 MPa
- **1903 British Imperial Standard**: 229 x 114 x 76mm (9 x 4.5 x 3 in)

#### Portland Cement Mortar (introduced ~1824, widespread from ~1850s)
- **Compressive strength**: 750-2500+ psi (5-17 MPa) depending on type
  - Type N: 750 psi (5.2 MPa)
  - Type S: 1800 psi (12.4 MPa)
  - Type M: 2500 psi (17.2 MPa)
- **Compared to lime mortar**: 50-250 psi (0.3-1.7 MPa)
- **Properties**: Sets faster; harder; less vapour-permeable; more brittle than lime
- **Issue**: Too-strong mortar with soft bricks causes brick damage (mortar should be softer than brick)

#### Wall Construction
- **Early Victorian**: Solid walls, 1.5-2 bricks thick (345-460mm / 13.5-18 in)
- **Late Victorian (from ~1870s)**: Cavity walls begin to appear
  - Outer skin: 1 brick thick (102.5mm)
  - Cavity: 50mm (2 in)
  - Inner skin: 1 brick thick (102.5mm)
  - Total: ~255mm (10 in)
- **Cavity wall purpose**: Prevent moisture penetration to interior
- **Bonding**: Flemish bond (decorative facades), English bond (structural walls), Stretcher bond (cavity walls)

Sources: [Imperial Bricks Sizes](https://www.imperialbricks.co.uk/guidance/everything-you-need-to-know-about-imperial-brick-sizes/), [Cavity Wall - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavity_wall), [Smith Heritage - Victorian Building Guide](https://smithheritagesurveyors.com/guide-to-buying-a-victorian-building/)

### 4.2 Damp Proof Course

- **Compulsory in London from 1875**
- **Material**: Two or three layers of slate built into wall above external ground level
- **Alternative materials**: Bitumen, jute, hessian
- **Air bricks**: Introduced in late Victorian period; clay bricks installed in walls and under floors for ventilation

Sources: [History of DPC - Preservation Treatments](https://preservationtreatments.co.uk/a-brief-history-of-damp-proof-coursing/), [Damp Proofing 1880 to Current - Cavitech](https://cavitech-uk.com/blog/damp-proofing-methods-from-1880-to-current-day/)

### 4.3 Windows

#### Sash Windows with Plate Glass
- **Plate glass**: Available after 1845 (glass tax removed); thick, heavy, could be made in large sheets
- **Effect on design**: Fewer glazing bars needed; two-over-two or one-over-one configurations replace six-over-six
- **Typical Victorian sash**: ~630mm x 1500mm per sash; overall ~900mm wide x 1200-1800mm tall
- **Glazing bar**: Single central bar common (vs. multiple Georgian bars)
- **Glass thickness**: ~3-4mm for plate glass (thicker than crown glass)

Sources: [Victorian Sash Windows Guide - Sash Window Specialist](https://www.sashwindowspecialist.co.uk/a-guide-to-sash-windows-through-the-victorian-era/), [Cast Plate Glass in Victorian Period](https://victorianweb.org/art/architecture/iron/plateglass.html)

### 4.4 Cast Iron and Wrought Iron

#### Cast Iron (columns, decorative elements, compression members)
- **Compressive strength**: 400-800 MPa (very strong in compression)
- **Tensile strength**: 100-200 MPa (weak; brittle failure)
- **Ratio**: Compressive strength 3-5x tensile strength
- **First structural use**: 1790s textile mills; Ditherington Flax Mill (1796) first cast-iron-framed building
- **Applications**: Columns, railings, gates, lamp-posts, conservatories, decorative panels
- **Note**: Brittle; not suitable for beams in tension/bending

#### Wrought Iron (beams, ties, tension members)
- **Tensile strength**: ~200-300 MPa (similar to mild steel)
- **Properties**: Malleable, ductile; similar strength in tension and compression
- **Applications**: Horizontal beams, bridge members, ties, straps
- **Used in combination**: Cast iron columns (compression) + wrought iron beams (bending/tension)
- **Superseded by steel**: Late 19th century

Sources: [19th-Century Structural Ironwork - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/structural-ironwork/structural-ironwork.htm), [Wrought Iron - Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/wrought-iron), [Cast Iron - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron)

### 4.5 Timber

- **Material**: Imported Baltic softwood (first-growth forest, superior quality to modern timber)
- **Floor joists**: ~200x50mm to 225x75mm (8x2 to 9x3 in); smaller than modern equivalents
- **Common Victorian joist**: ~125x75mm (5x3 in) for typical domestic spans
- **Joist spacing**: 350-400mm (14-16 in) centres
- **Floorboards**: Softwood (pine), ~150mm (6 in) wide, ~25mm (1 in) thick

Sources: [Timber Floors - Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/timberfloor/timberfloor.htm), [Right Survey - Floor Joist Span Tables](https://www.rightsurvey.co.uk/floor-joist-span-tables-for-surveyors-floor-construction/)

### 4.6 Services (Late Victorian)

- **Gas lighting**: Iron gas pipes run within walls/floors from ~1840s
- **Plumbing**: Lead and iron pipes; gravity-fed cold water from cisterns
- **Drainage**: Glazed clay drain pipes; trapped gullies
- **Coal fires**: Every room had a fireplace; cast iron fire surrounds

## 5. JAPANESE TRADITIONAL

### 5.1 Structural System

#### Post-and-Beam (No Load-Bearing Walls)
- **System**: Wooden columns (hashira) support horizontal beams (hari) and roof
- **No nails**: Traditional construction uses no metal fasteners or adhesives
- **Rationale for no nails**: Nails cause moisture ingress and rot in Japan's humid climate; nailless joints flex safely during earthquakes
- **Post spacing**: One tatami length apart (~1820mm / 6 ft)
- **Diagonal braces**: Sujikai for lateral stability

#### Timber Joinery Types
- **Tsugite**: End-to-end timber splicing; geometric locks that strengthen under load
- **Shiguchi**: Right-angle connections (post-to-beam, beam-to-brace)
- **Specific joints**:
  - Kata-sage-ari: "One-sided drop-down dovetail" for tie-beam to post
  - Kanawa-tsugi: Beam splicing joint for long spans
- **Materials**: Typically Japanese cypress (hinoki), cedar (sugi), or pine (matsu)

Sources: [Japanese Carpentry - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_carpentry), [Arch2O - Japanese Wood Joints](https://www.arch2o.com/how-japanese-wood-joints-work-without-nails-the-ancient-art-of-kigumi-in-modern-architecture/), [Japan Objects - Japanese Joinery](https://japanobjects.com/features/japanese-joinery)

### 5.2 Modular Dimensions (Tatami Module)

#### Tatami Mats
- **Standard size**: 910 x 1820mm (3 x 6 ft / 3 x 6 shaku) -- ratio 1:2
- **Legal minimum**: 1 jo = at least 1.62 m2 (900 x 1800mm)
- **Thickness**: ~55-60mm (2.2-2.4 in)
- **Material**: Compressed rice straw core, woven rush (igusa) surface
- **Room sizing**: Rooms described by number of tatami mats (e.g. 4.5-jo, 6-jo, 8-jo)
- **Regional variations exist** (Kyoto mats slightly larger than Tokyo mats)

#### Room Sizes (Standard)
| Room | Mats | Dimensions |
|------|------|-----------|
| 3-jo | 3 | 2730 x 1820mm |
| 4.5-jo | 4.5 | 2730 x 2730mm |
| 6-jo | 6 | 2730 x 3640mm |
| 8-jo | 8 | 3640 x 3640mm |
| 10-jo | 10 | 3640 x 4550mm |

Sources: [Tatami - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatami), [Japan Guide - Tatami Rooms](https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e2007.html), [Washitsu - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washitsu)

### 5.3 Sliding Screens

#### Shoji (Translucent)
- **Width**: ~910mm (3 ft) -- one tatami width
- **Height**: ~1800mm (5 ft 11 in) traditional; ~1900mm (6 ft 3 in) modern
- **Construction**: Wooden lattice frame covered with translucent washi paper
- **Tracks**: Slide in two parallel wood-groove tracks between posts

#### Fusuma (Opaque)
- **Width**: ~900mm (2 ft 11 in)
- **Height**: ~1800mm (5 ft 11 in) traditional; ~1900mm (6 ft 3 in) modern
- **Thickness**: 20-30mm (0.8-1.2 in)
- **Construction**: Lattice wooden understructure, cardboard layer, paper or cloth on both sides

Sources: [Shoji - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoji), [Fusuma - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusuma)

### 5.4 Engawa (Veranda)

- **Width**: Typically ~900-1000mm (3-3.3 ft) -- narrow compared to Western porches
- **Construction**: Timber-framed, continuous with interior floor level
- **Purpose**: Transitional space between interior and garden
- **Floor**: Polished timber boards running perpendicular to the building

Sources: [Big Sand Woodworking - Engawa](https://www.bigsandwoodworking.com/osakikamijima-engawa/)

### 5.5 Roof Construction

- **Tiles** (kawara): Curved clay tiles, interlocking; weight ~55-65 kg/m2
- **Thatch** (kayabuki): Thick reed or grass; steep pitch; ~45-60 degrees
- **Structure**: Complex timber truss system; deep eaves for rain/sun protection
- **Eave overhang**: Typically 600-900mm (2-3 ft)

## 6. MIDDLE EASTERN / ISLAMIC TRADITIONAL

### 6.1 Mud Brick / Adobe Construction

#### Brick Dimensions
- **Typical adobe brick**: ~250 x 400 x 125mm (10 x 16 x 5 in) -- varies regionally
- **Composition**: Clay/mud, straw (chopped), water; sun-dried
- **Compressive strength**: ~1-4 MPa (much lower than fired brick)

#### Wall Construction
- **Typical wall thickness**: 250-800mm (10-31 in); most commonly 300-500mm (12-20 in)
- **Thermal mass**: Thick walls absorb heat during day, release at night
- **Height limitation**: Typically 1 storey, ~3m (10 ft) high
- **Exception -- Shibam, Yemen**: Towers 5-11 storeys; walls taper (thicker at base, thinner above)
  - Lower walls: ~500-800mm thick
  - Upper walls: Progressively thinner; bricks decrease in size on upper floors
  - Reinforced with wooden beams at intervals

Sources: [Adobe - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe), [Earth Architecture - Middle East](https://eartharchitecture.org/?cat=6)

### 6.2 Courtyard House

- **Layout**: Inward-facing rooms arranged around central courtyard (principle of introvert)
- **Courtyard**: Central open space serving as "lung of the house" -- light, ventilation, private outdoor space
- **Room proportions**: Small by modern standards; minimal external windows
- **Wall heights**: ~3-4m per storey; courtyard walls often 4-6m to ensure privacy from neighbours
- **Flat roof**: Used as additional living/sleeping space (rooftop terrace)

Sources: [Sustainability in Islamic Architecture - EcoMena](https://www.ecomena.org/sustainability-islamic-architecture/)

### 6.3 Wind Towers (Badgir)

- **Typical height above roof**: 5m average; range 0.3-5m; tallest example 33.35m (Dowlatabad, Yazd)
- **Plan dimensions**: Typically ~3 x 3m (10 x 10 ft)
- **Height overall**: Up to 7m (some sources up to 15m above ground floor)
- **Construction**: Brick, same as walls
- **Configuration**: Multi-sided (usually 4-sided); open at top on 2 or 4 sides
- **Internal divisions**: Radial internal walls dividing shaft into vertical sections
- **Function**: Catches prevailing wind, channels cool air down into rooms; also creates stack effect for hot air extraction
- **Openings**: Face different directions to capture wind from varying angles

Sources: [Windcatcher - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windcatcher), [BADGIR - Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/badgir-traditional-structure-for-passive-air-conditioning/)

### 6.4 Mashrabiya Screens

- **Construction**: Turned wood pieces assembled without nails into geometric lattice patterns
- **Material**: Typically hardwood; regional variations
- **Function**: Privacy screening, light filtering, evaporative cooling (water jars placed behind screen), ventilation
- **Form**: Projecting oriel window (cantilevered bay); adds floor space to upper storeys
- **Patterns**: Range from simple geometric grids to complex interlocking patterns following Islamic geometric principles
- **Lattice spacing**: Varies by function -- tighter spacing for more privacy, wider for ventilation
- **Projection**: Typically 300-600mm (1-2 ft) from wall face

Sources: [Mashrabiya - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashrabiya), [Architizer - Modern Mashrabiya](https://architizer.com/blog/inspiration/stories/cultural-lattice-modern-mashrabiya-architecture/)

### 6.5 Flat Roof Construction

- **Primary structure**: Palm trunk beams (or tamarisk/sidr) spanning between walls
- **Beam spacing**: ~400-600mm centres
- **Secondary layer**: Split canes or reeds laid across beams
- **Tertiary layer**: Woven palm mat
- **Plaster/waterproofing layers** (bottom to top):
  1. Wet mud/earth plaster with straw and lime (~20-30mm) -- cements structure
  2. Dry earth/ash/chalk fill (~100-150mm thick) -- applied dry to minimise shrinkage
  3. Top coat: Lime plaster (Al-Qadad process) for waterproofing
- **Load capacity**: Limited; not designed for heavy point loads

Sources: [Nazareth Village - Roofs](https://nazarethvillage.com/about/research-and-archaeology/the-houses/roofs/), [Gulf Architecture](https://catnaps.org/islamic/gulfarch2.html)

## 7. MATERIAL PROPERTIES BY PERIOD

### 7.1 Brick Strength Over Time

| Period | Type | Compressive Strength | Notes |
|--------|------|---------------------|-------|
| Tudor (1500-1600) | Handmade, wood-fired | ~10-15 MPa | Irregular, variable |
| Georgian (1714-1830) | Handmade, improved kilns | ~15-20 MPa | More consistent |
| Victorian handmade | Handmade, coal-fired | ~18 MPa (mean 18.23 MPa) | Still variable |
| Victorian machine-made | Machine-pressed | ~20-35 MPa | Much more uniform |
| Modern (post-1903) | Machine-made, standardised | ~20-160+ MPa | Engineering bricks at top end |

Sources: [ResearchGate - Compressive Strength of Historical Bricks](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283785629_A_Comparative_Study_on_the_Compressive_Strength_of_Bricks_from_Different_Historical_Periods), [BIA Technical Notes](https://www.gobrick.com/media/file/3a-brick-masonry-material-properties.pdf)

### 7.2 Mortar Strength Comparison

| Type | Compressive Strength | Tensile Strength | Vapour Permeability | Flexibility |
|------|---------------------|------------------|--------------------| ------------|
| Historical lime mortar | 50-250 psi (0.3-1.7 MPa) | Very low | Very high | High |
| Hydraulic lime mortar | 250-500 psi (1.7-3.5 MPa) | Low | High | Medium |
| Portland Type O | 350 psi (2.4 MPa) | Low | Medium | Medium |
| Portland Type N | 750 psi (5.2 MPa) | Medium | Low | Low |
| Portland Type S | 1800 psi (12.4 MPa) | Medium-High | Low | Low |
| Portland Type M | 2500 psi (17.2 MPa) | High | Very low | Very low (brittle) |

**Key rule**: Mortar must always be softer than the masonry unit it bonds. Overly strong mortar damages softer historic brick.

Sources: [Craftsman Blog - Lime vs Portland](https://thecraftsmanblog.com/lime-mortar-vs-portland-cement/), [German Smear Specialists - Mortar Strengths](https://www.germansmear.com/blog/understanding-mortar-strengths-in-historical-and-modern-brickwork)

### 7.3 Historical Timber Properties

| Property | Green Oak | Seasoned Oak | Baltic Softwood (Victorian) | Modern Softwood (C16/C24) |
|----------|-----------|--------------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| Density (kg/m3) | ~800-1000 | ~600-700 | ~450-550 | ~350-500 |
| Bending strength (MPa) | ~30-40 | ~40-60 | ~30-45 | 16-24 (grade dependent) |
| Compressive strength (MPa) | ~15-20 | ~25-35 | ~20-30 | 17-21 |
| Moisture content | ~60-80% | ~12-20% | ~15-20% | ~12-20% |
| Notes | Easier to work; joints tighten on drying | Stronger but harder to work | Superior first-growth quality | Lower quality plantation timber |

### 7.4 Glass Properties by Period

| Type | Period | Thickness | Max Pane Size | Surface Quality | Production |
|------|--------|-----------|---------------|-----------------|------------|
| Crown glass | 15th-18th C | 1.5-2.5mm (variable) | ~560mm (22 in) diameter | Brilliant, slightly uneven; "bulls-eye" at centre | Blown and spun disc; edges used for panes |
| Cylinder/broad glass | 17th-19th C | 2-3mm (variable within pane) | Larger than crown | Good but less brilliant than crown | Blown cylinder, cut and flattened |
| Cast plate glass | Late 18th-19th C | 3-6mm | Very large sheets possible | Polished both sides; some optical defects | Cast on iron surface, ground and polished |
| Float glass | 1959 onward | 2-25mm (controlled) | Virtually unlimited | Perfect; uniform; no distortion | Molten glass on tin bath |

Sources: [Float Glass - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_glass), [Roger Mears Architects - Glass and Glazing](https://www.rmears.co.uk/our-publications/glass-and-glazing/), [Building Conservation - Plain Glazing](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/glazing/glazing.htm)

### 7.5 Iron Properties

| Property | Cast Iron | Wrought Iron | Mild Steel |
|----------|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Carbon content | 2-4% | <0.1% | 0.1-0.3% |
| Compressive strength | 400-800 MPa | ~200-300 MPa | ~250-400 MPa |
| Tensile strength | 100-200 MPa | ~200-300 MPa | ~250-400 MPa |
| Behaviour in tension | Brittle failure | Ductile | Ductile |
| Behaviour in compression | Excellent | Good | Good |
| Structural use | Columns, compression members | Beams, ties, tension members | Universal (replaced both) |

Sources: [Building Conservation - Structural Ironwork](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/structural-ironwork/structural-ironwork.htm), [Wrought Iron - Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/wrought-iron)

## 9. TIMELINE OF KEY CONSTRUCTION INNOVATIONS

| Date | Innovation | Impact |
|------|-----------|--------|
| Pre-1000 | Rubble stone + lime mortar | Standard for all masonry |
| ~1200s | English glass industry begins | Glazed windows for wealthy |
| ~1350 | Crown-post roof trusses | Wider spans without aisles |
| ~1400s | Timber-frame jettying | Upper floor overhang in towns |
| 1477 | Clay tile size regulated | 267x159x16mm standard |
| ~1500s | Brick becomes common (Tudor) | Chimneys, decorative patterns |
| 1571 | Brick size standardised | 229x114x57mm |
| ~1670s | Sash windows invented | Vertical sliding; crown glass |
| 1738 | Welsh slate naming system | Industrial slate quarrying begins |
| 1784 | Brick tax imposed | Bricks made larger to reduce count |
| ~1789 | Steam-polished plate glass | Larger window panes possible |
| 1824 | Portland cement patented | Stronger, faster-setting mortar |
| 1845 | Glass tax removed | Plate glass becomes affordable |
| ~1850s | Machine-made bricks | Uniform, consistent quality |
| ~1870s | Cavity walls introduced | Moisture barrier between skins |
| 1875 | DPC compulsory (London) | Slate DPC required in new builds |
| ~1890s | Steel replaces iron | Universal structural material |
| 1903 | British brick standard | 229x114x76mm imperial standard |
| 1959 | Float glass invented | Perfect flat glass; end of crown/plate |
